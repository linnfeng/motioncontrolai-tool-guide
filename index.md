How to Choose an AI Video Tool: A Practical Checklist for Creators
==================================================================

## Why choosing an AI video tool is harder than it looks

Every AI video tool advertises the same three things: it turns text into video, it turns an image into video, and it produces results in seconds. Those claims are all true and all fairly useless when you are trying to decide which one to keep. The differences that actually affect your work show up only after the first twenty minutes of use, and most comparison pages never mention them.

This is a checklist built from the questions that separate tools in practice. It is written for creators, small marketing teams and freelancers who need a repeatable way to make short video, not for researchers benchmarking model architecture.

## 1. What exactly can it take as input?

Input support is the first thing to check, and vendors describe it inconsistently. A useful classification has four levels: text only, text plus a still image, text plus image plus a motion clip, and the ability to work from a sequence of keyframes. Each level unlocks different creative options.

- Text only produces generic footage. It is fine for abstract backgrounds and establishing shots.
- Text plus image gives you a specific subject, which is the minimum for anything featuring a person, product or character.
- Text plus image plus motion clip lets you control movement rather than describe it. This is the level at which repeatable character animation becomes possible.
- Keyframe sequence support matters for multi-shot projects where the same character must appear several times.

If your work depends on a recurring character, a recurring product, or a consistent visual style, anything below the third level will force you into prompt gymnastics that never fully converge.

## 2. How consistent is the output across repeated generations?

Consistency is the property that decides whether a tool is usable for anything longer than five seconds. Test it honestly: generate the same concept five times and compare the results side by side. Look at the face, the wardrobe, the colour palette and the camera height.

A tool that produces one beautiful result out of five attempts is a demo. A tool that produces usable results four times out of five is a tool. A tool that produces usable results every time, with small variations, is the one you can build a schedule around.

## 3. What are the real duration and resolution limits?

Published limits are often the free tier rather than the model. Check the maximum clip length on the plan you would actually buy, the output resolution, and the frame rate. Ten seconds at 720p is workable for social content. Four seconds at 1080p is not, however good the frames look.

## 4. Does it handle motion, or only appearance?

Many tools render a convincing still and then animate it with a generic drift. Real motion means the movement you asked for is the movement you get: a turn is a turn, a walk cycle is a walk cycle, and timing is preserved. This is where reference-driven tools separate themselves, and where you should look for evidence that a motion reference is accepted rather than merely advertised.

## 5. What happens to the rights, and what happens to your inputs?

Read the terms rather than assuming. You need to know whether you own the output, whether the tool trains on your uploads, whether commercial use is included in the plan you are on, and whether there is an attribution requirement. These are ordinary commercial questions and a reputable service will answer them in writing.

A practical red flag is a tool that requires you to upload confidential client material before it will tell you anything about ownership. If the client work cannot be shared publicly, the tool is not usable for it regardless of output quality.

## 6. How long does a render take, and what does it cost?

Generation time matters more than the headline number suggests. A tool that takes forty seconds per clip and never fails will beat a tool that takes eight seconds and fails once in three, because you will finish the edit rather than the render queue. If credits are charged per generation, include your expected retry rate in the monthly estimate.

## 7. What is the export and editing story?

Check whether the tool exports a clean video file, whether there is a watermark on free plans, and whether the resolution can be matched to the platform you publish to. Also check whether the tool keeps a history of your generations, which saves a lot of time when you want to make one change to a take you liked.

## 8. Is there a usable free path?

Most tools offer a free tier with limits, and that is enough to evaluate. Use the free tier for the whole of your test period rather than switching to a trial of the paid plan, because the free tier is what most people will live with long term. ## Where free tiers mislead

Free tiers are usually generous in one dimension and stingy in another. Some give unlimited generations at low resolution. Some give a small number of high resolution generations. Some allow commercial use but keep a watermark. Read the tier description as a table rather than as a headline, and note the limits that would actually block your work: resolution, duration, watermark, and commercial rights.

A useful test is to imagine your most demanding normal week. How many clips, at what length, at what resolution, with what rights? If the free tier covers that, you may not need to pay at all for months. If it does not, you know the exact ceiling of what you are buying.

## How to evaluate a tool you are already using

The fastest evaluation is a controlled comparison. Take one subject, one movement and one framing. Produce five takes in tool A and five in tool B, with the same reference material. Compare identity consistency, artefact rate, and the time from request to usable file. Anything you cannot measure after twenty takes is a preference, not a finding.

Write the result down. Tool comparisons are rarely revisited, and a short note about what worked prevents you from re-running the same experiment next quarter.

## Common mistakes when picking a tool
- Choosing on demo quality. Demos are curated, short and unusually lucky. Test on your own material instead.
- Ignoring the retry rate. A tool with a sixty percent success rate can still be the better choice if failures are fast, but only if you measure it.
- Forgetting export limits. A tool that cannot produce the resolution your platform needs will force a re-render somewhere else.
- Overlooking rights. The cheapest tool is expensive if the output cannot be used commercially.

## A 30-minute test you can run today

Pick one short project you actually need. Find a clean still image of your subject and a ten-second motion clip of the movement you want. Generate five takes. Compare them for identity consistency. Check the duration, resolution and watermark. Read the terms for ownership. That half hour will tell you more than any feature list, and it costs nothing but the setup time.
